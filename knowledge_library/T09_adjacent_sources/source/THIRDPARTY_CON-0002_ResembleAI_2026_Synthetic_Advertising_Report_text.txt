       METHODOLOGY

       2026 Synthetic Advertising Report
       Based on 37,768 scanned ad files

       How this report was built: All figures come from Resemble Detect scans of ads pulled from Metaʼs public
       Ad Library. Detect checked every image and video for AI generation or manipulation. The dataset covers:

         ●    37,768 image and video files from 15,433 ads, run by 2,298 Facebook and Instagram pages

         ●    509 companies across 26 industries, found by searching each company name in the Ad Library in
              September 2026

         ●    A second collection in September 2026 covering nine prediction market, sportsbook and crypto
              brands, scanned for audio as well as visuals

         ●    Headline rates use only ads a brand ran from its own page: 28,463 files from 389 brands. Ads from
              other pages that matched the search are reported separately.

         ●    Every rate is a floor. The main collection was scanned for visual manipulation only, and audio alone
              caught 62 of the 142 fakes in the betting and crypto collection.

       Each file is classified by:

         1.   Verdict: a file counts as AI when Detect returns a Fake verdict

        2.    Advertiser type: whether the brand itself ran the ad, or another page using its name

        3.    Content: what each fake shows and how it is styled, labeled for all 3,216 fakes in the main collection

       Placement data is not reported. Meta keeps stopped US commercial ads out of the public Ad Library, so
       placements for most ads in the study could not be recovered after the scan.

       TOPLINE                                                                                   SECTION 01

       More than half of companies used AI-generated content.
       For a few, it was the majority of the ads.
       Detect flagged 3,379 of 37,768 ad files 8.9%. On brand pages the rate was 6.9%, but use was
       uneven: 167 brands ran none, while 13 brands used AI in more than 40% of their ads.




              A small group of advertisers were heavy generative AI users. Temu 64 of 100 files),
              Wayfair 56 of 100, Motel 6 50 of 80) and Lufthansa 50 of 94) each used AI in more than
              half of their sampled ads. The 185 brands with two or more AI ads averaged 12.5% of total
              ads containing AI, meaning those who used AI used it about a dozen times on average.

       INDUSTRY                                                                                 SECTION 02

       Consumer goods and electronics used the most Gen
       AI content, fast food did not
       All 26 industries ran AI ads. Rates ranged from 13.4% in consumer packaged goods to 2.4% in quick service
       restaurants. Regulated categories sat below the 6.9% average: insurance, banking and pharma all came in
       at 5.0% or lower.




              Regulated categories used AI least. Banking 3.8%, nonprofits 3.3%) and pharma 2.8%
              sit at the bottom alongside fast food. Consumer goods, electronics and fitness sit near the
              top, all categories built on product and lifestyle imagery.

       WHAT THE FAKES SHOW                                                                   SECTION 03

       Many AI ads depict hyper realistic looking people
       Seven in ten fakes were photoreal, and 46% showed a person. The most common subject was a realistic
       model, followed by product shots and scenes. Only 1 in 9 fakes was stylized enough that a viewer would
       read it as AI.




              Brands fake polish. Bait pages fake people. On brand pages, 2% of fakes imitated
              customer footage. On bait pages it was 11.2%, used to make clickbait look like a real
              personʼs story.

       WHAT THE AI AUDIO WAS                                                                        SECTION 05

       Most AI audio was voice, combined with real footage
       Of 67 fakes flagged on audio, 57 were synthetic voices and 10 were music or sound effects. 54 of the 57
       voices played over video Detect rated as real, so the picture was real and the voiceover was not. By eye
       and ear, these were generally not deceptive or intended to be deceptive, and were used primarily as an
       editing technique.




              Not all undisclosed AI is the same. A generated backing track and a generated
              spokesperson could be meaningfully different to different viewers. People can decide for
              themselves whether AI music matters to them. A synthetic voice presented as a real
              person has a lot more potential to come across as deceptive or misleading than a
              voiceover describing a scene.

       A prominent YouTuber found his background and
       body used in an ad
       On September 23, YouTuber Elliot Choy saw a Kalshi ad built from his 2025 apartment-move video. The
       script, room and framing matched his. The man on screen did not: AI had replaced Choy, who is Asian
       American, with a white man who doesnʼt exist. Kalshi told NPR the ad was old, that it no longer runs
       AI-generated ads, and that it was reviewing its relationship with the agency that made it.

       Even while legal, lack of disclosure in ads
       could be problematic
       Elliot Choy found out because he recognized his own apartment. Recognizing yourself or your
       apartment in an ad shouldnʼt be how the public learns that AI was used in advertising.

        In our data, 0.06% of AI files disclosed it in the ad copy. Waiting for advertisers to volunteer has not
       worked. Disclosure has to be built into each step between the model and the screen.
